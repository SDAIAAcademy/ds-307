Where definitions differ

Two definitions of one measure differ in four places

Grain

What is counted: trips, timepoint arrivals, riders, cards.

Rule

Where it is measured and the tolerance: first stop or terminus, early allowed or not.

Filter

What enters the denominator: cancelled trips, partial trips, holidays.

Period

Which dates: Gregorian month, Hijri month, a Sunday week or an ISO week.

Use it as a checklist. Two figures that disagree differ in at least one of these four. Find which before you look for a bug.

These four headings are the vocabulary of the diagnosis in Lab 1.1, where "rule" is split into "point measured" and "tolerance". Arithmetic errors and bad data exist, but they are rarer than definitional differences, and they show up only after the definitions match.

The biggest step

Counting timepoints, not trips, asks a different question

Per trip, at the first stop

"Did the bus leave when the timetable said?" The rider waiting at the start can plan on it.

Per timepoint arrival

"Was the bus on time along the way?" Delays build up, so later timepoints pull the figure down.

Neither is wrong. Planning's 66.0% is the honest answer to its own question. The fault is the shared name.

Ask: which question does Mashael need answered every Sunday? Usually the trip-level one for reliability at the start, read beside the cancellation rate. Keep the debate short; Session 2 resolves it by giving each definition its own name.

Why it matters

A disputed number spends the meeting on the number, not the decision

  • Three figures invite each team to quote the one that suits it
  • Every new report adds a definition nobody wrote down
  • An agent asked "what was on-time performance?" will pick one, and not say which
  • The fix is not a fourth report: it is one catalogue, one owner per metric

The agent line foreshadows Day 4: a conversational agent has no way to know which of three definitions is meant unless the semantic layer tells it. Ask whether anyone has seen a meeting go this way.

The 60-minute transfer window is Wadi Noor's fare rule (a transfer within 60 minutes is free), so the journey definition borrows it. A different window is a different metric, with a different name. Session 3 shows the non-additivity with numbers.

Anatomy of a definition

A definition someone else can compute has eight parts

  1. Name and the question it answers
  2. Formula: one aggregate, or a ratio of two
  3. Grain: what one counted row is
  4. Filters: what is in and out
  1. Time logic: which date, which calendar, which week
  2. Unit of the result
  3. Owner: a person and their role
  4. Limits: what it must not be used for
The test. Give it to a colleague: if they compute your number without asking you anything, it is a definition.

The catalogue template in the labs has these eight parts plus the table the formula runs on. The peer checklist, W.lint(), checks that each part is present and has the right shape; the colleague checks that it means what it says. Both are needed for the LO2 evidence.

An entry of the catalogue

The journeys definition, as the catalogue holds it

- name: journeys
  question: How many journeys did riders make, counting a transfer as part of one journey?
  formula: SUM(journey_start)
  table: boarding_linked
  grain: one card tap, journey_start = 1 when the card made no tap in the 60 minutes before
  filters: none
  time_logic: service_date of the tap; the 60-minute look-back crosses midnight
  unit: journeys
  owner: Dhuha Al-Rowais, network planning manager
  limits: a return trip within 60 minutes counts as a transfer

Point at time_logic: a tap shortly after midnight can be linked to one before it, so the look-back crosses the day boundary while the tap is counted on its own service date. Point at limits: it names a known undercount (quick returns), which protects the reader from over-reading the number.

Owners and change control

An owner decides what the metric means; a change gets a new version

  • The owner answers questions about the definition and approves changes
  • A change that moves the number is announced, dated and versioned
  • A second meaning is a second metric with its own name and owner
  • The board's measure stays, renamed terminal_on_time_rate, owned by Yasmeen

This is how the three figures are resolved without anyone losing: on_time_rate (Tawfiq), timepoint_on_time_rate (Dhuha) and terminal_on_time_rate (Yasmeen) are three metrics with three names. The dashboard and the agent use the names. The change log itself (pull requests on metric files, review in continuous integration) is DS-408's subject; here it is a dated line in the catalogue.

Proof, not belief

A definition reconciles when its formula, run as written, gives the source total

for d in defs:
    got = W.measure(con, d, *WEEK)
    W.check(got == counts[d["name"]], d["name"])
  • Run the formula on the table the definition names, for the period
  • Compare with the count taken directly from the source
  • Add a sum test: taps by route plus taps without a route equal all taps

The sum test matters on Day 3: in October some taps have no route, and a definition that silently drops them under-reports. "Taps without a route are counted" is written in the filters line of the taps definition for exactly that reason.

Ratios

A network rate is a ratio of sums, not a mean of route rates

RouteDepartedOn timeRate
A1009090%

Network15012583.3%
71.7%
mean of the three route rates

Not the network

The mean gives route B's ten trips the weight of route A's hundred.

The toy example of Lab 1.3; participants compute it by hand first. On the real September network the gap is smaller: 80.7% as a ratio of sums against 79.9% as a mean of the twelve route rates, because routes are closer in size. The rule does not change with the size of the gap.

When the mean is right

A mean of ratios answers "how does a typical route do?"

  • Network performance: ratio of sums, each trip counted once
  • The typical route: mean of route ratios, each route counted once
  • Both are legitimate; they are two metrics with two names
  • A dashboard that averages a column of percentages has chosen the second without saying so

The last bullet is the common way this goes wrong: an export of route rates, then AVERAGE in a spreadsheet. Ask participants to name a report at their own workplace that averages percentages.

From Lab 1.3. A card that travels every week is counted four times in the sum and once in the four-week count. Any total of a distinct count must be computed again over the longer period, never summed. Semantic layers mark such metrics as non-additive for this reason.

Flows and snapshots

Flows add over time; snapshots do not

Flow

Taps, trips, complaints: events in a period. A month is the sum of its days.

Snapshot

Active passes, buses in service, open complaints: a state on a date. A month is a chosen day, or an average of days, never a sum.

State the rule. A snapshot metric's time logic names the day it is taken: the last day of the period, or the mean of daily values.

The capstone's fares area has a snapshot: active 30-day passes on a date. Participants will meet it there. Ask for one more snapshot from the room's own work: headcount, open tickets, stock on hand.

04
Metric trees

A metric tree links one goal to the drivers that teams can move

Goalweekly card taps
=
Timetablescheduled trips
×
Delivery1 − cancellation rate
×
Demandtaps per departed trip
Guardrail. Complaints per 10,000 taps: it watches for harm a driver could do, such as crowding more riders onto each bus.

Check the algebra with the room: scheduled trips times one minus the cancellation rate is the departed trips; times taps per departed trip is the taps. A tree whose nodes do not combine back to the goal is only a list of related numbers. Each node is a catalogue metric.

Two kinds of tree

Multiply for rates and volumes, add for segments

Multiplicative

Taps = trips × (1 − cancelled) × taps per trip. Changes combine as products; log changes add.

Additive

Taps = North + Central + East. Each segment's change adds to the total; Day 3 drills down this way.

Use both. Multiply to find which driver moved; add to find where it moved.

The anomaly lab on Day 3 uses the additive form: the drop in weekly ridership is split by depot, then route, then day. The multiplicative form is today's lab.

NodeOwner

The guardrail moved from 27.7 to 23.1 complaints per 10,000 taps between the two weeks (Lab 1.4): no warning. A guardrail without a stated direction is a decoration. The owners come from the catalogue, so the tree and the catalogue cannot disagree.

What goes wrong

A tree misleads when its drivers overlap or do not reconcile

  • Two drivers that count the same thing double its effect
  • A driver measured at another grain or period breaks the product
  • A driver nobody owns is a number nobody acts on
  • A tree with no guardrail rewards crowding, skipping stops or cutting short trips

Reconciliation is the test: multiply back and compare with the goal, every week. Lab 1.4 checks it to a millionth of a tap. If a team proposes a driver "on-time rate", ask how it multiplies into taps: it does not, so it belongs beside the tree as a guardrail or a separate goal.

The national standard

The NDMO standards ask for analytics KPIs, measured and registered

  • The Business Intelligence and Analytics domain has 5 controls and 10 specifications
  • It asks for a plan, prioritised use cases, validation of their outcomes, KPIs and a register
  • A governed catalogue is the register of what each number means

For this course

Each dashboard is a use case: validate it, and record the metrics it shows with their owners.

Sources: NDMO Data Management and Personal Data Protection Standards, Business Intelligence and Analytics domain (controls BIA.1 to BIA.5). Do not quote clause wording; say what it asks in plain words, as on the slide.

Checkpoint

Which driver belongs in the tree?

The tree is taps = scheduled trips × (1 − cancellation rate) × taps per departed trip. A colleague proposes adding "on-time rate" as a fourth driver. What do you say?

Add it: punctuality drives demand
Replace the cancellation rate with it
Keep it beside the tree, as a guardrail or its own goal
Leave it out of the review
Why. The drivers of a multiplicative tree must multiply back to the goal. On-time rate does not appear in the identity, so it would break the reconciliation. It can still be watched beside the tree.

Answer: C. Some participants will argue punctuality causes demand. Agree, and point out that a causal link is a hypothesis to test, not a factor of an identity.
